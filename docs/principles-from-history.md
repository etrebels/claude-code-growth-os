# Principles from history, applied to go-to-market

History's operators left a usable manual. Stripped to their load-bearing ideas, the lives of people who built, led, and endured under pressure turn out to be operating principles in costume — the same leverage, momentum, and resilience the [science companion](principles-from-science.md) draws from physics and biology.

The first three sections draw from one well-documented life: Winston Churchill, as told in William Manchester's biography *The Last Lion* (Vol. 1 *Visions of Glory*; Vol. 2 *Alone*; Vol. 3 *Defender of the Realm*, completed by Paul Reid). The fourth, [Campaign](#campaign), draws from the military canon and the research on conflict, and keeps only the half of "business is war" that holds up. Each principle has a plain-language explanation, a **verified, attributed quote** (or, where no quote survives screening, a description instead), and a short worked go-to-market example. They're a thinking aid for anyone running a motion in this kit — not prescriptive, not brand-specific. **Bring your own sources; this is a pattern to copy.**

> **On sourcing — Churchill is the most misquoted man in the language.** Every Churchill quote below was checked against the [International Churchill Society](https://winstonchurchill.org) and the Churchill Project (Hillsdale College). The popular fakes are excluded on principle: *"Success is going from failure to failure without loss of enthusiasm,"* *"Success is not final, failure is not fatal…,"* and *"If you're going through hell, keep going"* have **no source** in the 2.5M+ words by and about Churchill — do not repeat them. The same discipline applies to every figure: an inspiring quote you can't source is a liability, not an asset. (*"He mobilized the English language and sent it into battle"* is Edward R. **Murrow** about Churchill — not Churchill's own line.)
>
> **The military canon gets the same screening.** *"There is no substitute for victory"* is **Douglas MacArthur**, to a joint meeting of Congress on 19 April 1951 — not Churchill. *"No plan survives first contact with the enemy"* is a later compression of Helmuth von **Moltke**, whose essay "Über Strategie" (1871) says something narrower: *"Kein Operationsplan reicht mit einiger Sicherheit über das erste Zusammentreffen mit der feindlichen Hauptmacht hinaus"* — no plan of operations extends with any certainty beyond the first encounter with the enemy's main force. *"Amateurs talk tactics, professionals talk logistics"* has no primary source: the usual credit to Omar Bradley is undocumented, and an earlier, longer form attributed to Gen. Robert H. Barrow (1979) rests on a single secondary report — use the idea, not the line. And the French admiral Villeneuve's supposed remark that at Trafalgar *"every captain was a Nelson"* is legend, with no primary source; Nelson's own Trafalgar Memorandum, quoted in [Campaign](#campaign), is the documented thing.

## Conviction

**Solitary foresight — be right before it's popular, on an earned read.** Manchester's striking claim is that Churchill's finest hour was the 1930s "wilderness," not the war: out of office and mocked as a warmonger, he warned of German rearmament and was vindicated by events, not by consensus. The detail that makes it operating advice: he ran a private intelligence network and had *better data than the government.* Foresight was earned, then defended — the opposite of stubbornness.
> "Never give in, never give in, never, never, never, never—in nothing, great or small, large or petty—never give in except to convictions of honour and good sense." — **Winston Churchill**, Harrow School, 29 October 1941.
**Example:** A contrarian channel bet (the channel everyone says is dead) is only worth holding if you've done more homework than the doubters. Earned conviction is an asset; posture dressed as conviction burns budget. Do the homework, then stop seeking permission.

**Keep going through the dip.** Compounding looks like failure right up until it doesn't. Churchill's private wartime shorthand for the daily discipline of continuing was "KBO."
> "We must just KBO," which, he explained, meant "Keep Buggering On." — **Winston Churchill**, to his private secretary, December 1941 (recorded by John Peck).
**Example:** Outbound and content are flywheels — little visible return for weeks, then sudden. The operator who KBOs through the quiet stretch outlasts the one who reads an empty first month as proof the channel is dead.

## Output

**Engineer your energy, not just your time — and recover actively.** Churchill's output was prodigious and ran on a *designed* daily system: morning work from bed, a disciplined afternoon nap he said gave him two working days inside one, dictation to relays of secretaries. Against depression he used not idleness but a change of muscle — painting and bricklaying.
> "I have had a delightful month building a cottage and dictating a book: 200 bricks and 2,000 words a day." — **Winston Churchill**, on his time at Chartwell, September 1928.
**Example:** Treat energy as the binding constraint. Put the hardest cognitive work (positioning, pricing, narrative) in your peak window and protect a real recovery block — it's not slack, it's what creates the second shift. When depleted, switch muscles rather than grinding a tired brain through a pipeline review.

**Bias to action.** Churchill governed by a flow of short, dated minutes and reserved a bright printed label — **ACTION THIS DAY** — for the few things that genuinely couldn't wait. His standing "Brevity" memo to the War Cabinet (1940) ordered officials to compress reports to their essential points so decisions could be made fast. *(Both are documented practice from the Churchill Archives — described here, not quoted, to avoid putting non-verbatim words in his mouth.)*
**Example:** A leader's scarcest export is momentum; most deals die of slow no's, not bad ones. Keep an "Action This Day" list to a true few and force them today — the label only works because it's rare.

## Persuasion & proportion

**Mobilize the words — communication is the work, not the decoration.** In 1940 the speeches held a nation together, and they were not improvised gifts: Churchill laboured roughly an hour over every minute he delivered. A correct decision no one is moved to act on is inert.
> "He mobilized the English language and sent it into battle." — **Edward R. Murrow**, broadcaster, on Churchill (Murrow's line, *about* Churchill).
**Example:** The pitch, the launch post, the board update, the renewal conversation are *built artefacts* — drafted, cut, rehearsed — not bolted on after the "real work." Spend the disproportionate hour on the few sentences that must land.

**Magnanimity in victory — win without poisoning the next round.** The moral Churchill placed at the front of his war memoirs is a whole operating philosophy in four words.
> "In War: Resolution. In Defeat: Defiance. In Victory: Magnanimity. In Peace: Goodwill." — **Winston Churchill**, the moral of *The Second World War* (1948).
**Example:** The close is the first interaction of the next relationship, not the last of this one. Win the negotiation, then stop — don't run up the score. The reference, the expansion, and the renewal all live on the other side of magnanimity.

## Lessons from war: compete within the rules, fight only your own weaknesses

The sections above draw on one life. This section states the rule first; the military canon and the conflict research appear only where they independently agree. (The canon drawn on: Sun Tzu, Clausewitz, Nelson, Lincoln, MacArthur, Eisenhower.) It is needed because "business is war" is the most common metaphor in go-to-market and it is only half right. The discipline is the half that holds: initiative, a clear aim, concentration, speed, morale, supply. Treating a person as the enemy is the half that doesn't. So the frame comes first, then the principles.

### The market is a contest; the war is with yourself

**The market is a contest run within the rules, not a war.** Most of go-to-market looks less like a battlefield than a league season: the same opponents, the same officials, a long run of games, and a reputation that carries from one game to the next. So play it the way a good team plays. Play offense, train for it, play within the rules, respect the opponent, shake hands after, and treat the score as the consequence of playing well rather than the object. The distinction is practical: a team that plays for the scoreboard starts bending the rules to move it, and a team that plays to a standard finds the scoreboard moves anyway. The competitor is an opponent to study, respect and out-serve. Never an enemy to damage, and never the hero of a war story.
**Example:** After a lost bake-off, close graciously with the buyer and send the winning team a courteous note. Then review the loss by asking "did we serve the buyer's decision better?" before "why did we lose?" The first question finds the fix. The second finds a villain.

**Deal fairly with good-faith counterparts; meet the bad-faith actor with prudence, not retaliation.** A customer, partner or supplier dealing in good faith should come away with at least as much as they gave: price, scope and delivery that match, and hardship terms written at signature rather than invented in a crisis. Some counterparts show, by their conduct over time, that they are playing to take. Commitments change after you have acted on them, information you shared gets used against you, each person hears a different story. Meet that with prudence: terms in writing, a walk-away line set before the meeting, nothing exposed that you can't afford to lose, claims checked before you commit, and a clean exit when the guard costs more than the relationship is worth. Never retaliation, never deception, never a parting shot. Someone can act like an adversary without you becoming one. Robert Axelrod's tournaments (*The Evolution of Cooperation*, 1984) found that in repeated dealings the strategy that won was *nice* (never the first to break faith), *retaliatory*, *forgiving* and *clear*, and that envying the other side's gains was a losing habit. Keep three of the four. Replace *retaliatory* with withdrawing trust, enforcing the terms both sides already signed, or leaving. A consequence agreed in advance is not retaliation; a harm invented afterward to punish is. The way back stays open: see [the best moment to fix a hostile working relationship](principles-from-the-field.md#conflict--resolution).
**Example:** A reseller with "a large enterprise book" asks for exclusivity. Check the references and the book before you sign, not after. If the claims turn out to be inflated, honor the contract you signed to the end of its term, and don't renew.

**Not every situation is the same game. Read it before you choose the posture.** Whether a situation is a contest, a trade, or something to walk away from isn't a property of business. It depends on who is on the other side and what rules you are both under. Anatol Rapoport (*Fights, Games, and Debates*, 1960) sorted conflict into three kinds: in a fight the aim is to harm the opponent, in a game to outplay them within rules, in a debate to convince them. Go-to-market is games and debates. A fight in Rapoport's sense, where the aim is to harm the other side, is never the right posture toward a person; the only fight is with a condition (below). So read the situation with five questions, in this order. **Is the thing opposing you a person at all, or a condition** (inertia, fear, drift, "everyone in this market does it")? A buyer stuck in the status quo is a person held by a condition. **Who** are you dealing with, judged by their behavior over time rather than their words? (First contact counts as "not yet known.") **What rules and referee** exist: a signed contract, a regulator, a published procurement process, or none? **What is at stake**, and can it be undone? **Can you walk away** to another route to the same end? Then choose the posture before the tactic:

- **Service.** Your own team, and the partners who carry risk with you: the highest duty of care, open books, disputes settled privately and early.
- **Contest.** A good-faith opponent, shared rules, a referee, the same prize: compete as hard as the rules allow, measure against the standard rather than the rival, and take the result as a consequence.
- **Trade.** A good-faith counterpart exchanging value, and every first contact: fair measure both ways.
- **Guarded dealing.** A pattern of bad faith, no referee, or high stakes with someone unproven: terms in writing, the walk-away line set first, no naive trust.
- **Withdrawal (walking away).** The offer was refused, or the guard costs more than the relationship is worth, and there is another route: leave cleanly and keep every promise already made.
- **Defense.** You are under attack and can't or mustn't walk away: hold, protect your people and clients, use the proper legal or contractual channel, and keep offering settlement. Never retaliate, never deceive, never take the fight public.
- **Fight the condition (the inward war).** The only posture with an offensive, and it is never aimed at a person (next principle).

**Example:** Service: a partner and you both want the same named account; give them the account, put the boundary in writing, keep the partner. Withdrawal: a prospect keeps asking for unpaid pilot work after declining the proposal; say once, plainly, why you stop here, leave the door open on stated terms, and move to the next account. Defense: a former subcontractor passes off your method and contacts your clients; tell the clients the facts once without attacking anyone, write privately, then meet with a neutral witness, offer settlement at every step, and use the contract's dispute clause only if the six tests pass in writing.

Move between postures in a fixed order, not by mood. Check your own motive first (fear, pride, the wish to win the argument). Offer fair terms first. Count before you commit, and verify claims before any binding promise. Set the walk-away line before it is tested. Escalate privately before publicly, one step at a time (the [private-first ladder](principles-from-the-field.md#conflict--resolution)). Yield ground that can be replaced; never yield honesty, your people's safety, or a promise already made. Before any public confrontation (a lawsuit, a regulator complaint, a public correction) answer six questions in writing, adapted from the just-war tests: is the cause just, is this the proper forum, is the intent to protect rather than punish, is it the last resort, is success likely, is the response proportionate? Two conditions override all six: never against your own people or a partner who carries risk with you, and nothing deceptive or vindictive. Keep your word even to someone who misled you: decline to renew, don't break it. And re-read the situation after any breach, after any repair, and at every renewal. Postures move both ways, and the way back to good faith stays open.
**Example:** The same company can be your opponent in a live deal and your partner on an integration; Brandenburger and Nalebuff call this *co-opetition* (1996). Read the situation, not the logo: compete hard in the deal, keep your word on the integration, and don't let one bleed into the other.

**The only war worth the name is against your own weaknesses, and the conditions that make you quit.** What actually beats a go-to-market team is rarely a person. It is a condition: fear (the price not said, the call not made), drift, discouragement after a loss, the pull of "everyone in this market does it," and above all the status quo. The loss may more often be to no decision than to any rival — one vendor-adjacent analysis puts it at 40–60% (directional): Matthew Dixon and Ted McKenna (*The JOLT Effect*, 2022), from more than 2.5 million recorded sales conversations, report that share of qualified B2B deals ending in no decision rather than a loss to a competitor. *(DIRECTIONAL: a range from a named, vendor-adjacent source, not peer-reviewed. Cite it as a range, never as a point figure.)* A buyer stuck in the status quo is a person held by a condition. Serve them a clear case for acting, and turn the war on the condition, and on your own slowness, never on them.
**Example:** A deal stalls in "no decision." The fight-the-person reflex presses the buyer harder. The fight-the-condition read asks what is holding the buyer in place (no budget owner, no visible cost of waiting, a failed project last year) and builds the case that answers it, while asking what in your own process let the deal drift.

Every principle below reads two ways: as offense in the contest (go to the buyer, make the ask, set the terms of the next contact) and as a campaign against your own weak points. Keep the vocabulary inside the team. War words don't belong in a pitch, a deck, or anything a buyer reads.

### Move first

**Take the initiative; don't wait for inbound.** An operation that waits for buyers to arrive, maintains what it has, and reacts to whatever comes in isn't being careful. It is slowly falling behind. Military doctrine treats the offensive as a principle of war in its own right; the 2001 edition of the US Army's field manual FM 3-0 frames it as seizing, retaining and exploiting the initiative. *(Described, not quoted: the principles of war are reworded from one edition to the next.)* Offense is a posture, not a channel. A renewal secured early or an expansion opened counts as a win; an account drifting toward non-renewal counts as a loss.
**Example:** Count the share of this week's conversations you started against the ones that came to you, and set a floor. A new initiative is easiest to kill in its first weeks, so protect it then, before a quiet month gets read as proof it doesn't work.

**When the next step is unclear, get closer to the decision (close the distance).** When the signals go dark (the champion stops replying, the deal's status can't be read) the instinct is to wait, or to send something soft. Nelson's standing instruction to his captains before Trafalgar was the opposite: when in doubt, get close.
> "But, in case Signals can neither be seen or perfectly understood, no Captain can do very wrong if he places his Ship alongside that of an Enemy." — **Horatio Nelson**, Trafalgar Memorandum, "Victory, off Cadiz, 9th October, 1805."
**Example:** A late-stage deal goes quiet for two weeks. Another nurture email is the passive move. Asking the decision-maker for twenty minutes ("where does this stand, and what would make it a no?") is the active one, and you will rarely go far wrong with it. In the market, "alongside" means in front of the decision, never against the buyer.

**A plan acted on beats a better plan discussed.** The value of planning is the thinking it forces, which is what lets you act when events move. The plan document is the least valuable thing it produces.
> "I tell this story to illustrate the truth of the statement I heard long ago in the Army: Plans are worthless, but planning is everything." — **Dwight D. Eisenhower**, remarks to the National Defense Executive Reserve Conference, Washington, 14 November 1957 (quoting an Army saying, not coining it).
**Example:** A plan discussed in two reviews with no conversation, proposal or ask attached gets executed or cut at the third. The test isn't whether the plan is good. It is whether anything in this week's Top 3 is the action the plan calls for.

### See it clearly, then concentrate

**Get a clear picture of the problem, and run a system rather than trust to chance.** Most of what you act on in a deal is uncertain, and the uncertainty doesn't shrink by itself.
> "War is the province of uncertainty: three-fourths of those things upon which action in War must be calculated, are hidden more or less in the clouds of great uncertainty." — **Carl von Clausewitz**, *On War* (tr. J. J. Graham, 1873), Book I, ch. III.
**Example:** Discovery exists to shrink that three-fourths. Map the buying committee, the decision process and the cost of doing nothing before a deal goes into the forecast. Then ask the same qualification questions on every deal, so that a win or a loss teaches you something instead of feeling like luck.

**Concentrate on the one thing that decides it.** Clausewitz's *center of gravity* (the hub that everything else depends on, where the concentrated effort should fall) carries over intact. *(Paraphrased: the wording is still to be checked against the Graham text.)* Spread effort evenly and nothing moves. Put most of it on the point that decides the outcome, and the rest follows.
**Example:** In each deal, name the single biggest blocker (the security review, the budget owner you haven't met) and put the week's effort there. Across the business, put most of the hours on a few accounts or one segment, and hold the side channels at maintenance level.

**Fix one recurring weakness at a time, and stay on it until it stops coming back (lay siege to it).** The weakness that costs the most is rarely complicated. It is a simple thing done inconsistently, and the misses add up.
> "Everything is very simple in War, but the simplest thing is difficult. These difficulties accumulate and produce a friction which no man can imagine exactly who has not seen War." — **Carl von Clausewitz**, *On War* (tr. J. J. Graham, 1873), Book I, ch. VII, "Friction in War."
**Example:** Pick one: a next step booked before every call ends, follow-up inside 24 hours, the deal note written the same day. Name it in a sentence, give it one owner and a date, track how often it recurs, and stay on it until it stops coming back. One at a time; a siege on five things is a siege on none.

### Time, aim and cost

**Time is the scarcest resource.** A territory or a segment can be re-entered. A quarter can't. Speed isn't hurry; it is refusing to let decisions sit.
> "There is no instance of a country having benefited from prolonged warfare." — **Sun Tzu**, *The Art of War* (tr. Lionel Giles, 1910), II.6.
**Example:** A pilot with no end date is a prolonged campaign: it consumes the team and decides nothing. Fix the date and the success criteria at signature, follow up the same day, send the proposal inside 48 hours, and treat a blocked priority as a 24-hour problem, with a workaround or a named escalation by tomorrow.

**Write down what winning means before you start (name the victory before the campaign).** Before a push (a quarter's focus, a pilot, a fix) write one sentence on what "won" means, and call a partial result partial.
> "War's very object is victory, not prolonged indecision. In war there is no substitute for victory." — **Douglas MacArthur**, address to a joint meeting of Congress, 19 April 1951.
**Example:** In a campaign against your own weakness, the victory is the weakness gone, not "improved." In the contest, the victory you name is the customer's result (their measured outcome at day 90), and revenue follows as its consequence. The moment the number itself becomes the object, the rule-bending starts; see *where the metaphor breaks*, below.

**Count the cost first.** The worst deals are often the ones whose cost was visible before anyone committed.
> "Thus it is that in war the victorious strategist only seeks battle after the victory has been won, whereas he who is destined to defeat first fights and afterwards looks for victory." — **Sun Tzu**, *The Art of War* (tr. Lionel Giles, 1910), IV.15.
**Example:** Before a deal passes discovery, write a one-page cost-to-finish: delivery hours, your own time, the top three ways it fails, and the walk-away line. Win the deal you can deliver and decline the one you can't. If you are outgunned on price or relationship, change the frame (a narrower scope, a partner, a different way in) rather than play a contest you knew you would lose.

### People and supply

**One owner and a written intent, so the team acts when the leader is absent.** Nelson's line above came from a memorandum issued to his captains *before* the battle, so that each could act rightly when the signals failed. *(Described: the memorandum is a statement of intent, not a script.)* A motion with shared ownership has no owner, and a team that knows only the instructions, not the intent, stops when the leader leaves the room.
**Example:** Every open deal has exactly one owner and a one-sentence intent: "we want X for the buyer because Y." When the founder isn't on the call, an associate who knows the intent can make the right call, and when unsure falls back on the Nelson default: get closer to the decision.

**Wholehearted few over many half-committed.** The army on paper is not the army in the field, and the gap between them is where campaigns are lost.
> "I am told that over 160,000 men have gone into your army on the Peninsula. When I was with you the other day we made out 86,500 remaining, leaving 73,500 to be accounted for. … If I am right, and you had these men with you, you could go into Richmond in the next three days." — **Abraham Lincoln**, letter to George B. McClellan, 13 July 1862.
**Example:** The pipeline on paper isn't the pipeline in the field. For each open deal, ask whether the champion has done anything that cost them: introduced the budget owner, shared real data, put the next meeting on the calendar. If not, the deal is an absentee. A smaller pipeline of committed accounts beats a forecast padded with maybes. The same goes for who you put on critical work: choose for readiness, not headcount.

**Morale is a resource: spend it, and replenish it.** UK military doctrine lists the maintenance of morale as a principle of war in its own right, beside offensive action, flexibility and sustainability, and an old maxim credited to Napoleon weighs the moral against the material at three to one. *(Both described, not quoted: the doctrine is reworded between editions, and the English versions of the maxim vary.)* Morale comes from conviction in the work, not from cheerfulness, and the condition most likely to end a campaign early is discouragement after a loss.
**Example:** Track the team's energy the way you track cash; a recovery block missed three days in a week is a flagged shortfall, not a rounding error. After a lost deal, allow a short cooling-off window, then a written review within the week with one named change, so the loss teaches instead of lingering. (see [Morale](principles-from-the-field.md#morale)).

**Protect the cash, capacity and energy the work runs on (honor the supply line).** Cash, delivery capacity and the operator's own recovery are what every campaign runs on — and a motion that outruns them collapses at the moment it looks strongest. *(No quote: the famous line about amateurs and logistics has no source; see the note at the top of this page.)*
**Example:** Before a push, check three numbers: runway in days, delivery hours against signed scope, and who absorbs the extra load. Protect the accounts you already hold before buying new ones. Retention is the supply line for next year's growth.

> **Where the metaphor breaks, and the evidence for it.** War teaches discipline well and ethics badly, and the second half is measurable.
>
> - **Rivalry makes people cheat.** Kilduff, Galinsky, Gallo and Reade, "Whatever It Takes to Win: Rivalry Increases Unethical Behavior," *Academy of Management Journal* 59(5), 2016, pp. 1508–1534: using archival data from professional soccer leagues plus experiments, they found that framing a competitor as a rival raised unsporting behavior, deception and willingness to use unethical negotiating tactics, by raising what felt at stake. *(SOLID: peer-reviewed; read through the abstract and summaries.)* That is the cost of playing for the scoreboard, and the reason the contest is played within the rules.
> - **Customers aren't enemies, and competitors are opponents, not enemies.** A metaphor carries its values with it, and war brings a harm-the-other ethic that markets don't need. Name a competitor's strengths plainly; don't run them down. No war stories about beating a rival, in public or in private.
> - **Deception doesn't carry over.** *"All warfare is based on deception"* (Sun Tzu, I.18) is quoted here only to reject it. Surprise (an unexpected insight, an offer nobody else made) carries over. A misleading claim never does.
> - **Markets are often positive-sum.** The same company can be competitor and complementor at once, and even the war canon ranks the bloodless win highest: *"supreme excellence consists in breaking the enemy's resistance without fighting"* (Sun Tzu, III.2). In the market, that is being the obvious choice before anyone calls a bake-off.
> - **Some counterparts do act as adversaries.** The answer is prudence (guarded dealing, defense, or withdrawal), never war on the person.
